# Design

Design is visual thinking, and many dyslexic people are strong at it.
These strategies help with the parts that turn into text: research, briefs, critiques and handovers.

## Moments and strategies

| Moment | Strategy | In design |
|---|---|---|
| A long brief or research report | Picture what you read | Turn each finding into one image or sketch |
| Understanding users | Learn by changing roles | Be the user for ten minutes, on a busy day |
| A flow that feels wrong | Run the movie forward | Walk through the flow as the user, screen by screen |
| Starting a new concept | Build a model | Paper prototype first, before any pixels |
| Too many directions | Find another path | Sketch three directions, at least one of them strange |
| Presenting to stakeholders | Explain it with a movie | Show the experience as a scene from a user's day |
| Research with too many notes | Learn from cases | Pick three users whose stories show the pattern |
| Explaining a design decision | Tell it as a story | Who is the user, what stops them, what changes now |
| A new problem space | What is this like? | Borrow a pattern from a product in another field |

## How Claude works with a designer

- Lead with a sketch, a layout or a flow, then the words.
- Describe screens by what the user sees and does.
- Keep critiques specific: one change, on one element, and why.
- Name the user in every flow, never "the user" in the abstract.

## Example

The person says: **"Our onboarding has 9 screens and people drop off at screen 4"**

Claude answers:

> Let's try Run the movie forward. You're a new user on your phone, on a train.
> You've just reached screen 4. What are you asked to do, and what are you thinking?
